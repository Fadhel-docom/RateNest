A PRACTICAL PRICING GUIDE

How to price a freelance project without guessing.

A clear quote starts with the work, the rate your business needs, and the risks you can explain—not a number chosen because it feels comfortable.

Build a quote with the free project pricing calculator →

1. Define the work before estimating it

Write down the deliverables, what is excluded, who supplies materials or content, how feedback will be handled, and what counts as completion. A vague brief creates a vague estimate. If important details are missing, list your assumptions in the quote or clarify them before committing to a fixed price.

2. Start from a sustainable hourly rate

Your rate should help cover the income you need, business expenses, time off, and work that cannot be billed to clients. Do not divide your target income by every hour you work: sales, administration, learning, and gaps between projects take time too.

Use the freelance hourly rate calculator to estimate a baseline from your own numbers. It does not estimate personal taxes or predict what a client will pay, so account for obligations that apply to you separately.

3. Estimate the effort in parts

Break the project into stages such as discovery, preparation, production, communication, revisions, testing, and handover. Estimate each stage separately, then add them up. Include client meetings and project administration when they take your time, even if they do not produce a visible deliverable.

  1. List the stages: make the work visible rather than estimating from the final deliverable alone.
  2. Estimate each stage: use comparable past work when available, and note assumptions where it is not.
  3. Check the total: ask what could cause rework, waiting, extra communication, or a change in scope.

4. Calculate the quote transparently

Hours × your rate+Chosen buffer + direct costs

For example, suppose you estimate 20 hours at $60 per hour. Labour is $1,200. If you choose a 15% uncertainty allowance on labour ($180) and include $150 of direct project expenses, the example quote is $1,530.

The percentage is an example, not a universal recommendation. Choose an allowance that fits the uncertainty and your agreement—or use 0% when no allowance is appropriate. Direct expenses should not be counted twice if they are already covered elsewhere in your rate.

Try your own hours, rate, expenses, and buffer in the project quote calculator.

5. Protect the scope in writing

A price alone cannot prevent misunderstandings. Before work begins, make the agreement specific enough that both sides can tell what is included and what would change the price.

  • Deliverables and exclusions: name the files, pages, features, formats, or outcomes included—and list important items that are not included.
  • Review and revisions: state how many review rounds are included, how feedback is collected, and what counts as a correction versus a new direction.
  • Client responsibilities: list the content, access, decisions, and approvals the client must provide, plus any dates your schedule depends on.
  • Milestones and payment: for larger work, split the project into reviewable outcomes with an amount and due point for each. Agree the payment schedule before work starts.
  • Change requests: explain that work outside the agreed scope needs a separate estimate, timeline update, and written approval before you begin it.

What to do when the client asks for more

  1. Pause the newly requested work; do not silently absorb it into the original price.
  2. Describe what changed and which agreed deliverable or assumption it affects.
  3. Estimate the additional hours, direct costs, price, and schedule impact.
  4. Send the revised amount and timeline for written approval, then continue under the updated agreement.

Example: your quote covers a five-page website and two review rounds. After approving the page structure, the client asks for a sixth landing page. That is a new deliverable, not automatically a correction to one of the five pages. Estimate the extra work, state whether the delivery date changes, and get approval before building it.

If the brief is too uncertain to define deliverables or estimate the work responsibly, do not force a fixed price. Offer a separately scoped discovery stage or a time-boxed hourly phase, then quote the better-defined work.

If the work is recurring rather than a one-off deliverable, compare the arrangement with the retainer pricing calculator. A retainer should describe reserved capacity and service boundaries, not imply unlimited work.

For further reading: Upwork’s guide to estimating fixed-price projects discusses scope, tasks, milestones, and scope creep. Its milestone help article is specific to Upwork’s platform; payment-protection and review rules differ across services and direct contracts.

6. Review the effective rate after delivery

Once the project ends, compare the quote with the actual hours and costs. If the work took longer than expected, identify why: an inaccurate estimate, extra client requests, a slow approval process, or a missing task. Use that information to improve the next estimate instead of automatically applying the same buffer to every project.

This guide provides general business-planning information, not tax, legal, or financial advice. Rates and contract requirements depend on your situation and market.

Common questions about project pricing

Should I charge hourly or use a fixed project price?

Hourly billing can fit work where scope is uncertain or changes frequently. A fixed price can suit defined deliverables when you can estimate the work and agree on how changes are handled. Neither model removes the need to define expectations.

Is a buffer the same as profit margin?

No. Adding a percentage to labour is a markup-style allowance. A target profit margin is calculated differently, so do not label the buffer as a guaranteed margin or profit.

Should I include expenses in my quote?

Include direct costs the client price needs to cover, such as project materials or subcontractors. Make clear what is included and avoid counting a cost twice.

Can a calculator tell me what the market will accept?

No. A calculator makes your assumptions and arithmetic clearer. Client budgets, specialist experience, alternatives, the value of the outcome, and your market also affect what price is viable.
